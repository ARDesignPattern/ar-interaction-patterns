This study focuses on the empirical validation of interaction design decisions represented within the HMD-AR pattern system.

The research examined validation at two complementary levels: first, by studying the effects of specific design parameters on visitor experience; and second, by deploying related pattern-based interaction structures across different museum environments to investigate their reuse and adaptation in practice.

## 1. Evaluating specific interaction design parameters

Parameterized design patterns make it possible to describe not only recurring interaction structures, but also the design decisions that can vary between implementations.

One such parameter is spatial anchoring: where interface elements are positioned in relation to the visitor, the physical exhibit, and the surrounding environment.

A mixed-methods study compared different anchoring strategies for HMD-based museum AR, including body-anchored and exhibit-anchored interface configurations.

The study examined how these placement decisions influenced usability, cognitive workload, interaction behavior, and the overall visitor experience.

The results showed that spatial placement should not be treated as a purely technical implementation detail. Different anchoring strategies create different experiential and interaction characteristics, providing empirical evidence for treating anchoring as an explicit design parameter within reusable HMD-AR interaction patterns.

This study therefore represents a deeper validation of one specific design dimension within the broader pattern system rather than a validation of the entire system through a single experiment.

## 2. Evaluating reusable patterns across different museum contexts

The research also examined whether interaction structures represented in the pattern language could be reused and adapted across different real-world museum environments.

Related pattern-based interaction concepts were implemented and deployed in different natural history museum contexts, with the same underlying interaction logic adapted to different exhibits, spatial conditions, content, and visitor scenarios.

These deployments provided an opportunity to evaluate the patterns beyond a single prototype or exhibition setting. Rather than reproducing identical applications, the interaction structures were adapted through their parameters while preserving their underlying design intent.

Visitor studies and field deployments across these contexts provided evidence that the patterns could support the development of coherent HMD-AR experiences under different museum conditions.

The findings also helped identify the practical boundaries of reuse: patterns provide reusable design structures, but their parameters and implementation still need to respond to the spatial, narrative, educational, and curatorial characteristics of each exhibition.

Together, these deployments contributed further evidence for the adaptability and transferability of the pattern-based approach in real museum environments.

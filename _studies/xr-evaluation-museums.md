This study examines how HMD-based interaction can be evaluated in real museum environments, where visitors are often first-time users and interaction unfolds simultaneously inside the headset and within the surrounding physical exhibition space.

The evaluation approach developed progressively from practical field-observation methods toward a combination of structured observation and standardized self-report measures.

## 1. Observing HMD interaction in real museum environments

Evaluating HMD-based AR in museums presents a practical challenge: researchers need to understand both what visitors see and do inside the headset and how they physically move, react, and interact with the surrounding exhibition.

Early field studies therefore focused on establishing a workable observation setup for real museum conditions. The headset view was streamed to an external portable display so that researchers could follow the visitor's in-headset interaction while simultaneously observing their physical behavior in the exhibition space.

Structured observation sheets were used to record interaction events, difficulties, and recurring usability problems without repeatedly interrupting the visitor during the experience.

This approach was particularly useful for first-time HMD users, because many interaction difficulties were visible not only in questionnaire responses but also in hesitation, missed cues, repeated actions, spatial confusion, and other observable behavior during use.

The resulting evaluation setup provided a practical basis for studying HMD interaction under realistic museum conditions rather than relying only on laboratory-based testing.

## 2. Combining field observation with standardized evaluation instruments

As the prototypes became more complex, the evaluation approach was extended beyond direct observation to include standardized and adapted questionnaire measures.

In the study of playful locative interaction, the evaluation combined field-based observation with a playful-experience questionnaire adapted from prior work and a usability questionnaire derived from established usability measures. This made it possible to examine not only whether visitors could successfully interact with the system, but also how they experienced the interaction in terms of playfulness and usability.

Later work on game-mechanic-based interaction expanded this approach further by adapting four established questionnaires to capture multiple dimensions of the visitor experience. These measures were used alongside prototype testing to evaluate aspects such as usability, workload, engagement, and experiential qualities across different interaction concepts.

The purpose was not to replace field observation with questionnaires, but to combine complementary forms of evidence: direct behavioral observation helped reveal where and how interaction problems occurred, while standardized self-report instruments provided a more structured view of visitors' perceived experience.

Together, these methods formed a more systematic evaluation approach for comparing and refining HMD-based museum interaction designs across different prototypes and deployments.
